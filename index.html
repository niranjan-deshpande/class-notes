ReferenceDefinitions

Lec 1 · Locke

Iconic theory of meaning
The meaning of a linguistic item is a corresponding mental icon (an idea), or an operation on such an icon.
Being understood
Being made rationally intelligible: the hearer comes to know your mental states (beliefs, desires, goals) and how they rationalize each other and your action.
Empiricism
The view that all ideas are derived from experience.
Three touch-points
What a theory of language should explain: (1) language makes us rationally explicable to each other; (2) some of it is assessable as true or false; (3) meaning is systematic.
Functional vocabulary
Small words that don’t obviously refer to things in the world, e.g. the logical words and, or, not.
Compositionality
A language L is compositional iff for every complex expression e in L, the meaning of e is determined by the structure of e and the meanings of its constituents.
Semantics / syntax
Semantics: the study of meaning. Syntax: the study of which strings are grammatically well-formed in a language.

Lec 2 · Frege, Function & Concept

A priori / a posteriori
A belief is justified a priori iff its justification is completely independent of sense perception; a posteriori iff it depends at least partly on perception.
Analytic (Kant)
True by conceptual containment: the predicate is contained in the subject concept (“all bachelors are unmarried”).
Function
A rule associating each input (the argument) with an output (the function’s value for that argument).
Truth-functional connective
A word like not or and whose output truth value depends only on the truth values of the sentences it combines.
Content
How the world is according to a sentence; what can be true or false.
Force
What a speaker is doing with a content when they use a sentence: asserting it, wondering about it, hedging, etc.

Lec 3 · Frege, Sense & Reference

Reference (Bedeutung)
The thing an expression stands for. Both “Hesperus” and “Phosphorus” refer to Venus.
Sense (Sinn)
The mode of presentation of the referent, modeled on a descriptive condition. Sense determines reference.
Mode of presentation
The way an object is presented to a thinker, like one face of a mountain. Not a mental image; can be shared.
Cognitive significance
S and S′ differ in cognitive significance iff a rational thinker who understands both can believe S is true without believing S′ is true.
Frege’s Criterion
Expressions e₁ and e₂ have different senses iff there are sentences S, S′ that differ only by swapping e₁ for e₂ and that differ in cognitive significance.
Fregean Communication
Speakers communicate successfully with a sentence only if both grasp its sense.
Conception
All the information an individual associates with a term. Varies person to person; irrelevant to communication (unlike sense).

Lec 4 · Russell, On Denoting

Empty name / description
A name or description with no referent: “the present King of France,” “the largest natural number.”
Negative existential
A sentence denying existence, e.g. “The present King of France does not exist.”
Referential vs. quantificational NP
Referential noun phrases talk about particular things (“Mary”); quantificational ones express generalizations (“everyone,” “fifteen people”).
Theory of Descriptions
“The F is G” is true iff there is exactly one F and every F is G. Descriptions are quantifiers, not names.
Scope
The scope of an operator is what the operator applies to. Quantifiers (and descriptions) take scope; referring terms don’t.

Lec 5 · Russell, Acquaintance

Sense-data
Mental entities we are directly aware of: the mental counterparts of images, sounds, tastes. They account for what experience is like.
Acquaintance
Direct, infallible awareness, e.g. of sense-data: if it seems you’re acquainted with a sense-datum, you are.
Intentionality
Something has intentionality when it is about something else.
Proposition
The meaning of a sentence and the object of attitudes like belief. For Russell, an ordered structure of objects, properties, and relations: ⟨a, b, R⟩.
Logically proper name
A name that contributes an object itself to the proposition expressed.
Description Theory of Names
Every apparent name is really a covert definite description, except names for currently entertained sense-data.
Principle of Acquaintance
Every proposition we can understand must be composed wholly of constituents with which we are acquainted.

Lec 6 · Quine

Referential opacity
A context (like “believes that …”) where substituting co-referring terms can change truth value.
De dicto ascription
A belief ascription meant to identify both which object the subject is thinking about and how they think of it. Substitution invalid.
De re ascription
A belief ascription meant only to identify which object the subject is thinking about (“believes of x that …”). Substitution valid.
Notional / relational belief
Notional belief always involves a mode of presentation; relational belief would be a direct relation to an object with none.
Quantifying in
A quantifier outside an attitude verb binding a variable inside it: ∃x(Ralph believes that x is a spy).

Lec 7 · Perry

Indexical
An expression whose reference depends on the context of use: I, here, now, this, (some uses of) tall.
Essential indexical
An indexical in an explanation of action that can’t be replaced by a name or description without destroying the explanation.
De se belief
Self-locating belief, like Perry’s “I am making a mess,” tied to oneself in a way that connects directly to action.
Doctrine of Propositions
Propositions (1) are the objects of belief, (2) are true or false absolutely, (3) obey Frege’s criterion of difference of sense.
Relativized proposition
A proposition true or false only relative to a parameter (a time, a person, a world).

Lec 8 · Strawson

Meaning as use
The meaning of an expression is a rule for its use (not merely determined by use). Essentially social.
Type / token
A type is a class of more specific individuals; a token is a specific individual. An expression is a type; an utterance is a token.
Same use
Two utterances make the same use of an expression iff they refer to the same object (more generally, express the same truth conditions).
Entailment
S entails p iff whenever S is true p is true, and if S is not true p need not be.
Presupposition
Background taken for granted: p is implied by S and by every member of S’s family (its negation, conditional, possibility, question).
Attributive / referential use
Donnellan: “the φ” used about whoever is the φ (attributive) vs. used to pick out someone the speaker has in mind (referential).

Lec 9 · Kaplan

Downward entailment
A position is downward entailing if swapping in something more specific preserves truth (“I don’t have a pizza” ⇒ “I don’t have a pizza with anchovies”).
Character
Conventional meaning, what a competent speaker knows. A function from context to content.
Content
What is said: the proposition expressed in a context.
Context
A possible occasion of use, which fixes the interpretation of context-sensitive expressions. Formally, a tagged list (speaker, audience, place, time, demonstrata).
Circumstance
A possible situation at which a content is evaluated as true or false.
Metasemantics
The study of the facts in virtue of which expressions have their meanings (vs. semantics, the study of meaning).

Lecture 1Locke and the iconic theory of meaning

Locke, An Essay Concerning Human Understanding (1689), III.ii

Words mark ideas

For Locke, the goal of language is to be understood, which here means being made rationally intelligible. Not in a very deep sense: just “I had a goal, constraints, opportunities, so I acted as I did.” To understand someone is to know their mental states (beliefs, desires, goals, reasoning) and how those states rationalize each other and their behavior. Rationality matters a lot for Locke.

So words “mark ideas.” Ideas are the things we reason with: pictures before the mind that we manipulate in order to reason. Locke is an empiricist: all ideas come from experience.

  • Worry: it seems very hard to share ideas themselves. But perhaps you give me an image of your having the image.
  • Nice feature: it’s easy to see what it would mean for an idea to be right or wrong; we can point to false beliefs. So there are two ways to assess someone’s mental states: by the relations between them (do they rationalize each other?), and by their truth or falsity.
The Iconic Theory

The meaning of a linguistic item is a corresponding mental icon, or an operation on such an icon.

We can compose meaning-bearing elements to understand new sentences: different parts are different ideas, and some parts are operations on those ideas. To understand “Taylor Swift is watching a game at Harvard Stadium,” take your image of Taylor Swift and your image of the stadium during a game and smush them together.

Three touch-points for a theory of language

A philosophical theory of language should answer to three things, and Locke’s covers all three:

  1. Rational explicability. We use language to make ourselves intelligible to each other.
  2. Truth. Some of language is properly assessed as true or false.
  3. Systematicity. We understand novel sentences from the meanings of their parts and how they’re put together.

Locke’s project is about the rational, the semantic, and the systematic. It sets aside, e.g., the way poetry makes us feel.

Objections: Berkeley’s triangle and the word “not”

  • Berkeley. Locke has to say reference is fixed by resemblance. But a mental picture of a triangle is always of some specific triangle, and we can still reason about triangles in general.
  • Functional vocabulary (small words that don’t obviously stand for things, like and, or, not). And is easy: for “Mary and Jane ate a cookie,” combine the two images. Not is hard. What image corresponds to “Taylor Swift is not walking”?
    • Not a picture of her walking, since then it would mean the same as “Taylor Swift is walking.”
    • Not a picture of her walking with a bar through it. HandoutThat image doesn’t resemble any situation in the world.
    • Not a picture of her doing something else, because asserting the sentence doesn’t commit you to her doing anything in particular.
  • Or looks just as hard. HandoutSo do quantifiers (“fifteen,” “some,” “none”) and tense.

Frege’s alternative: start from systematicity

Locke took nouns and verbs as the paradigm and then had to fit everything else in. Frege starts from the other end: from systematicity (“an infinite use of finite means”) and from functional vocabulary like and and not. He asks what nouns and verbs must mean for that to work. Truth comes first; rationality is dealt with later.

Compositionality

A language L is compositional iff for every complex expression e in L, the meaning of e in L is determined by the structure of e in L and the meanings of the constituents of e in L.

Handout

Why “structure” is in the definition: “Iago loves Desdemona” and “Desdemona loves Iago” have the same parts but different meanings. Also defined: semantics (the study of meaning) and syntax (the study of which strings are grammatically well-formed).

Lecture 2Frege: function and concept

Frege, “Function and Concept” (1891)

Review: the three touch-points

  • Rational explanation is key for Locke: rational reaction as opposed to pure causation.
  • For language to be assessed as true or false, it has to be about the world. (It’s hard for music to be about something.)
  • Language must be systematic: an infinite use of finite means.

Locke started with rational explanation. Frege starts with systematicity.

Motivation: is arithmetic analytic?

For Kant, some a priori knowledge is unpuzzling because it’s just containment: “all bachelors are unmarried” is analytic. But math is weird. The concept “7 + 5” doesn’t seem to contain “12,” yet “7 + 5 = 12” is clearly a priori. So Kant calls arithmetic synthetic a priori.

Frege wants to show that arithmetic is analytic after all: its first principles can be proved from definitions using logical laws alone. But proofs written in natural language might smuggle in unnoticed assumptions. So he builds the Begriffsschrift (“concept-script”), a formal (higher-order) logical language that lets nothing through that wasn’t expressly assumed. That’s why systematicity matters so much to him.

Numbers vs. numerals. A numeral is the symbol we write; a number is the abstract object it refers to. So numerals stand to numbers as names stand to people.

Connectives as truth functions

A function is a rule taking an argument to a value. Negation flips a truth value, so it looks a lot like a function, and so does and:

not(x) = True if x = False; False if x = True
and(x, y) = True iff x = True and y = True

Take “Mary ran and Jane did not run.” If and is a function from two truth values to one, then the sentences it combines must refer to truth values. Sentences refer to the True or the False.

Predicates as functions, names as arguments

If “Mary runs” refers to the True, what are “Mary” and “runs” doing? Something has to output the True. Frege: “runs” is the function (from objects to truth values, giving True exactly for things that run) and “Mary” is the argument. In lecture this was presented as a free choice.

The same pattern shows up everywhere:

  • the capital of ( ) countries → cities; Germany ↦ Berlin
  • the husband of ( ) people → people

Force vs. content

  • Content: how the world is according to the sentence; what can be true or false.
  • Force: what the speaker is doing with that content, such as asserting it (committing to its truth), wondering about it (“I wonder… maybe…”), etc.

Frege wants to keep these apart, because force doesn’t pass through every operator. Asserting “Mary ate a cookie and Jane ran” commits you to “Mary ate a cookie”; asserting “Mary ate a cookie or Jane ran,” or “it’s not the case that Mary ate a cookie,” doesn’t. The same content appears without being asserted.

This is where the iconic theory fails badly. It’s fine with and but fails on not, because on the empiricist picture, having an image before the mind is believing it.

What Frege wants is a semantics you can plug and chug through to get truth values. Open question: how does any of this give us rational explanation?

Handout

Substitution and the President puzzle (sets up Lec 3). A function cares only about what its argument is, not how it’s picked out, so swapping co-referring terms should preserve truth. That works for “The President is in the White House; Trump is the President; so Trump is in the White House.” It fails for “The President is elected every four years; Trump is the President; so Trump is elected every four years.”

Lecture 3Frege: sense and reference

Frege, “On Sense and Reference” (1892)

Recall that Frege’s semantics is built from functions and the objects they take as arguments, so names are central. (On papers: the question should be central, with a clear criterion for an answer.)

Hesperus and Phosphorus: informative identities

Take an identity statement “a = b.” The “is” here is the is of identity, not the copula of “I am good.”

  • Hesperus is Hesperus.
  • Hesperus is Phosphorus.

If a name’s contribution is just its referent (the Reference View), both say the same thing about Venus. But the first is trivial and the second was an astronomical discovery. So what makes one analytic and the other empirical?

The Name View answers that “a = b” is about the names. Frege rejects it (two arguments in the reading).

Cognitive significance

Compare “Andy is a bachelor” with “Andy is an unmarried man.” The two have different associations but are analytically equivalent. Frege cares only about what bears on our rational capacities, our reasons for believing and acting, and not about associations.

Cognitive significance

S and S′ differ in cognitive significance iff a rational thinker who understands all the expressions in both can believe S is true while not believing S′ is true.

Sense as mode of presentation

Frege’s solution is sense: the mode of presentation of the referent, the way it is presented to you. Think of one view of a mountain as opposed to the mountain itself. HandoutHiking: “this mountain is this mountain” vs. “this mountain is the mountain I saw this morning.”

You can approximate a mode of presentation as an image, but modes of presentation are not mental images. The pill example:

  • Draw twice from a pile of indistinguishable pills, getting the same pill both times. The images are identical, but you have two modes of presentation, since you don’t know it’s the same pill.
  • Now follow one pill continuously with your eyes. The image may change, but there’s only one mode of presentation.

Modes of presentation can also be shared: two people can see the same view of a mountain. Images can’t. HandoutFrege’s telescope: the image in the lens is public, the image on each observer’s retina is private. On the (controversial) Frege–Schlick view, we can’t even ask whether the contents of two people’s minds are similar.

How sense determines reference

Generalize the model: a sense is like a descriptive criterion. “Hesperus” has a sense like “the first heavenly body visible in the evening sky”; Handout“Phosphorus,” “the last heavenly body visible in the morning sky.”

How does language attach to the world? A word is conventionally tied to a sense, the sense is a descriptive condition, and the referent is whatever satisfies it.

  • “H = H” is trivial: take the same sense on both sides and follow it out into the world. Of course it’s true.
  • “H = P” is informative: the criteria are different, so you have to check empirically that they pick out the same thing (they do).
Frege’s Criterion

Expressions e₁ and e₂ have different senses iff there are sentences S₁ and S₂ such that (1) they differ only in that e₁ is replaced by e₂, and (2) they differ in cognitive significance.

Sense and successful communication

Fregean Communication

Speakers communicate successfully using a sentence only if they both grasp the sense of that sentence.

  • Co-reference isn’t enough. Paderewski was a famous pianist who later became Polish prime minister. You say “Paderewski is a great musician”; I’ve only met him as a politician and tell him “your music is wonderful!” Same referent, yet communication failed. HandoutSimilarly, Hannah Smith: I form a true belief from your true claim, but about a different Hannah Smith.
  • Sharing all beliefs isn’t required. HandoutA conception is everything one person associates with a term; it varies person to person and is irrelevant to communication.

So sense is the middle ground: co-reference is necessary but not sufficient, and sharing all your beliefs is sufficient but not necessary. Frege does admit that senses vary between people (the “Aristotle” footnote) and calls this an imperfection of language. HandoutHe says it can be tolerated as long as the referent is the same, which sits uneasily with the communication principle.

Lecture 4Russell: on denoting

Russell, “On Denoting” (1905)

Handout

I have no notes for this lecture, so this section is written from the handout. The last part is from my Section 2 notes, which reviewed it.

Empty names and negative existentials

Some names and descriptions are empty: “the planet farthest from the Sun,” “the largest natural number,” or a name a journalist invents to attribute fabricated quotes. “The planet farthest from the Sun is larger than Pluto” seems like a whole thought, but if the description refers to nothing it’s semantically just “     is larger than Pluto.” Frege offers two answers: such sentences really are empty, so neither true nor false (Sense and Reference), or the description gets a dummy referent (Function and Concept).

The sharpest form of the problem is the negative existential:

  1. A subject–predicate sentence with a referring subject is true iff the subject refers to something that has the predicated property.
  2. “The present King of France does not exist” is such a sentence.
  3. It’s true.
  4. So “the present King of France” refers to something.

Meinong bites the bullet: the King “subsists” without existing. Russell wants to avoid this.

Referring vs. quantifying noun phrases

Referential noun phrases talk about particular things (“Mary,” “the professor for PHIL 147”). Quantificational ones express generalizations (“someone,” “fifteen people,” “everyone in this course,” “many students”). For Russell, “everything,” “nothing,” and “something” are defined from the primitive “C(x) is always true.” Quantification can’t be reduced to reference: “Every person in the room wears glasses” equals “Andre, Barbara, and Clyde wear glasses” only given the extra fact that they’re all and only the people in the room.

Quantified sentences have the shape Quantifier · Restrictor · Nuclear scope (“Every F G”), and “Some F G” can be defined as “not: all F not-G.”

The Theory of Descriptions

“The TF for PHIL 147 teaches a couple of sections” implies existence and uniqueness: there is exactly one TF, and that TF teaches a couple of sections.

Russell’s Theory of Descriptions

“The F is G” is true iff there is exactly one F and every F is G.

∃x (Fx ∧ ∀y (Fy → y = x) ∧ Gx)

This solves the puzzle. “The present King of France does not exist” means it is not the case that there is exactly one present King of France:

¬∃x (Kx ∧ ∀y (Ky → y = x))

There is no subject term left to refer to anything, so there’s no need for Meinong.

Scope: “The King of France is not bald”

The scope of an operator is what it applies to: compare 2(3) + 2 = 8 with 2(3 + 2) = 10, or “I saw the woman with binoculars.” Quantificational expressions enter scope relations; referential ones don’t. Since descriptions are quantifiers, “The present King of France is not bald” has two readings:

  • It’s not the case that the present King of France is bald. true: there is no such king
  • The present King of France is such that he is not bald. false

Compare “All that glitters is not gold.” On “The present King of France is bald,” Frege says it’s neither true nor false; Russell says it’s false.

Section 2 review

  • For Frege, “the King of France does not exist” comes out neither true nor false, but it’s obviously true. For Russell it is true, as the negation of an existence-and-uniqueness claim. (Does Frege have a response here?)
  • For Russell, quantifiers don’t have reference: “everyone” doesn’t refer to specific people. (Could quantifiers refer to the whole world, as though the world had the property of existing?)
  • Russell likes his view because it’s better ontologically (no subsisting non-existents), and perhaps epistemically.
  • Correspondence theory of truth: “S” is true iff S is among the true things. Open questions: what is Russell’s theory of what makes something true, and of how communication and mutual understanding happen, i.e. how words attach to objects (see Lec 5)?
Handout

Two problems for Russell to think about. Uniqueness: “I’m the daughter of a farmer” doesn’t imply you’re an only child. Desires: “Mary wants to squash the rat in her attic” becomes “Mary wants there to be exactly one rat in her attic and to squash it,” which implies she wants a rat in her attic.

Lecture 5Russell: acquaintance and description

Russell, “Knowledge by Acquaintance and Knowledge by Description” (1910)

Sense-data and acquaintance

Start with colour (or X-) constancy: when the light changes, my perception of my pencil and the underlying sense-data change, but perception at a higher level doesn’t really.

For Russell, what I’m most immediately aware of is sense-data. HandoutThese are mental entities, the counterparts of images, sounds, and tastes, that account for what experience is like. We are acquainted with sense-data, and acquaintance is infallible: seeing a pencil can go wrong, but there’s no appearance/reality gap for sense-data themselves.

That’s a substantive claim, though. The speckled hen: I see a hen with lots of speckles in the distance, and it seems to have an indeterminate number of them. Does the sense-datum have an indeterminate number of speckles?

Three accounts of intentionality

Something has intentionality when it’s about something. Three accounts so far:

  • Iconic theory: resemblance. The problem is that an image must resemble its object in some respects, or else everything resembles everything. Specifying which respects requires interpreting the image, and that defeats the purpose, since the theory was meant to explain what an idea is about.
  • Frege: descriptive criteria (senses).
  • Russell: descriptive and causal. A thought is about a thing because that thing causes the sense-data I’m aware of and I think descriptive thoughts about it. Issue: many things are causally responsible for my current sense-data, so how do we get the right description? (To think more about.)

Russellian propositions

Propositions are the meanings of sentences beyond the words: one proposition can be expressed in many languages, which makes them a good way to pin down what a disagreement is about. HandoutThey’re also the objects of belief. For Russell, a proposition contains the objects it’s about plus the properties and relations predicated of them, as an ordered structure:

⟨Sun, Moon, occludes⟩ ≠ ⟨Moon, Sun, occludes⟩

Order matters, since “the Sun blocks the Moon” ≠ “the Moon blocks the Sun.”

Names as disguised descriptions

What about fake names? A con artist introduces “Joe Smith, who’s excited about this opportunity.” How is the victim’s thought meaningful? Their proposition isn’t about Joe Smith but about “the man introduced to me as a backer.” The name is short for a description, so it doesn’t refer.

  • A logically proper name contributes an object itself to the proposition.
  • The test: if you can suppose the subject doesn’t exist and the proposition is still meaningful, the subject is a description, not a name.
  • Since we can always build Cartesian skeptical scenarios, this applies to everything outside our current sense-data.
Description Theory of Names

Every apparent name is really a covert definite description, except names for currently entertained sense-data.

What drives Russell: when it seems to me that I’m entertaining a proposition, I really am. I can be wrong about whether it’s true, but not about whether I’m thinking a contentful thought. And propositions contain what they’re about, so their constituents must be things whose existence is guaranteed, which means sense-data.

Principle of Acquaintance (handout)

Every proposition we can understand must be composed wholly of constituents with which we are acquainted.

Handout

Strictly, Russell thinks we are also acquainted with properties and relations (universals), so descriptions are built from sense-data plus universals. The description behind a name also varies between people and over time. That raises the communication puzzle: if each of us only entertains propositions about our own sense-data, two speakers never grasp the same proposition, so what makes communication succeed?

Lecture 6Quine: quantifiers and propositional attitudes

Quine, “Quantifiers and Propositional Attitudes” (1956)

Recall the three touch-points: rational explanation, systematicity, and aboutness. Quine is interested in two ways of ascribing belief, and in whether that implies a metaphysical difference (two kinds of belief).

Ralph and Ortcutt: substitution in belief reports

Ralph suspects the man in the brown hat is a spy. He thinks the man he saw at the beach (a pillar of the community) is not. Unbeknownst to Ralph, they are the same man, Bernard J. Ortcutt.

  1. Ralph believes that the man in the brown hat is a spy.
  2. The man in the brown hat is the man on the beach.
  3. ✗So Ralph believes that the man on the beach is a spy.

The inference fails, which is weird if functions don’t care about the way their argument is denoted. This is a failure of substitution, due to perspective (like sense). HandoutSuch a context is referentially opaque.

  • Frege’s solution: in belief reports, words refer to their customary sense (their “indirect reference”). The two descriptions have different senses, so the substitution never applied. This is intuitive: what motivates action isn’t what we’re referring to but how we think of it. Belief reports are about modes of presentation, not referents.
  • Russell’s solution: rewrite descriptions as quantifiers (“there’s exactly one man in a brown hat and he’s a spy”), and that doesn’t entail the beach version. Issue: since names are descriptions built from the believer’s own sense-data, we can’t characterize how Ralph is thinking in shareable terms. HandoutFregean senses, by contrast, are public.

De dicto vs. de re ascriptions

“Ralph believes that someone is a spy” can mean:

  • (a) just on general principles: there are spies, but he can’t point to one. Ralph believes ∃x(x is a spy)
  • (b) he has someone specific in mind. ∃x(Ralph believes x is a spy)

These motivate, and justify, different behavior. Compare “Ernest is hunting a lion”: no particular lion vs. “there is a lion such that Ernest is hunting it.”

De dicto ascription

A belief ascription is de dicto iff it’s intended to identify which object the subject is thinking about and how they’re thinking of it. “L believes that S can F.”

De re ascription

Only intended to identify which object the subject is thinking about. “L believes of S that he can F.”

The difference is whether the referent is introduced inside or outside the belief report. Co-referring terms can be substituted in de re ascriptions and cannot in de dicto ones.

Quine’s worry about de re belief

Can we say Ralph believes of Ortcutt that he is a spy? It seems so: if he believes that the man in the brown hat is a spy, he believes of that man that he’s a spy, and since de re allows substitution, he believes of Ortcutt that he’s a spy. By the same route from the beach, he believes of Ortcutt that he’s not a spy. So Ralph both does and doesn’t believe of Ortcutt that he’s a spy.

Sometimes we engage the world under a mode of presentation (notional belief). Quine also posits relational belief, a direct relation to the object with no mode of presentation (as “seeing” seems to allow substitution).

Handout

Why the incoherence depends on relational belief. The incoherence only bites if de re ascriptions report a distinct, purely relational belief. Then Ralph would stand in, and not stand in, one and the same direct relation to Ortcutt. If all belief is notional, the two de re ascriptions just involve two different modes of presentation, and there’s no contradiction. HandoutQuine also notes a second problem: de re ascriptions come apart from what Ralph would sincerely assert.

Kaplan: reducing de re to de dicto

Suppose all belief goes through a mode of presentation. Then a de re ascription just quantifies over modes of presentation (Kaplan 1968):

  • There is some MoP m such that Ralph believes, under m, that Ortcutt is a spy;
  • and there is some MoP m′ such that Ralph believes, under m′, that Ortcutt is not a spy.

We divorce the mode of presentation in the reporter’s words from the one quantified over and leave open which one Ralph is using. No contradiction. So there are still two kinds of ascription, which differ semantically, but only one kind of belief: notional.

Moral: a semantic difference doesn’t necessarily mean a metaphysical one. De dicto and de re are kinds of belief ascription; to claim they’re different kinds of belief takes a separate metaphysical argument.

Lecture 7Perry: the essential indexical

Perry, “The Problem of the Essential Indexical” (1979)

Self-locating belief

The paper is from 1979, close in time to Jackson’s Mary case, and the two are quite similar: in the shopper case you can be completely clear about all the facts and still be missing something. What you learn is a purely perspectival fact. What is that new knowledge, and is it an extra kind of fact? This is the challenge of self-locating belief.

De se belief

Belief connected to oneself in a way that is directly connected to action, the kind of belief Perry acquires in the shopper case.

The messy shopper and the Doctrine of Propositions

Perry follows a trail of sugar around a supermarket counter, looking for the shopper with the torn sack, and then realizes: “I was the shopper I was trying to catch.” What did he learn?

  • It clearly concerns Perry, but it’s distinct from “the shopper with the leaking bag is making a mess,” which he believed all along (Fregean distinctness).
  • So some special mode of presentation by which Perry is presented to himself is doing the work.
  • It can’t simply be the sense of “I am making a mess,” because that sentence means something different in someone else’s mouth. It isn’t absolutely true or false.

Perry’s target is the Doctrine of Propositions. Propositions:

  1. are the objects of belief;
  2. are true or false absolutely;
  3. obey Frege’s criterion of difference of sense.

We’d like a proposition that explains the change in behavior and satisfies all three. How could that work for indexicals?

Why no description can replace “I”

Try another sense for “Perry”: “the only bearded philosopher in a Safeway west of the Mississippi is making a mess.” That still doesn’t explain why he changes what he does. You need “…and that’s me!”, which is an indexical again. This is the essential indexical: replacing it with any other term destroys the explanation.

An indexical is an expression whose reference depends on context: pronouns, demonstratives, adjectives like “tall,” etc.

Nickel: linguistic vs. conceptual indexicals

Perry’s argument slips between different notions of “indexical.” Consider:

  • Every girl turned in her homework. “her” isn’t one person, but it isn’t indexical either
  • Perry learned that he was making a mess.
  • Perry learned that he, Perry, was making a mess.
Handout

What the examples show. Perry’s thesis is about a concept, the “missing conceptual ingredient” in the belief that explains action. But he argues for it using a linguistic category, indexical words. The examples pull these apart. The “he” in the second sentence is a bound pronoun, like “her” in the first, not an indexical. “He, Perry” isn’t indexical at all. Yet both correctly report what Perry learned and explain why he stopped. So the linguistic category isn’t what’s essential, and tying a debate about mental states to it is odd.

Frege: a private sense of oneself

Frege would say there is a special mode of presentation, a thought that leads immediately to action. You can’t grasp my version of it, since your thought can’t move my leg. Each person is presented to themselves in a special, primitive way that only they can grasp. HandoutFrege’s example is Dr. Lauben, who has to use “I” in a public sense (“he who is speaking to you”) to communicate.

Perry grants he has no knock-down argument against these private, limited-access propositions, but he rejects them anyway: he believes “only in a common actual world.” The metaphysical cost is too high.

Relativized propositions

When two people each think “I am making a mess,” they seem to learn the same thing. For Frege that can’t be right: different referents mean different senses and different thoughts.

The fix is to give up Absoluteness. Believing “Garber is President” seems to cause the same action at different times, so treat it as one proposition whose truth value changes over time, and index propositions to times. The proposal is to do the same for people: take the common structure (a relativized proposition with no particular person built in) and evaluate its truth against a particular context. HandoutA Fregean can already relativize to worlds, so this isn’t a deep departure.

From section: imagine two omniscient gods in different places. They know every proposition about the world, yet neither knows which god it is. Only something that just one of them could know would tell them apart.

Perry’s objection and Nickel’s reply

Perry: both Perry and another shopper watching him can believe that “I am making a mess” is true-for-Perry, but they act very differently. The context of belief need not be the context of evaluation.

Nickel: this assumes that believing p is the same as believing that p is true for me. But believing a proposition is different from believing it’s true relative to some parameter. The other shopper believes that Perry’s proposition is true relative to Perry; he doesn’t believe the proposition himself. So the objection doesn’t land.

Handout

Perry’s own positive view: the two shoppers are in the same belief state (which explains their similar behavior) but believe different things.

Lecture 8Strawson: on referring

Strawson, “On Referring” (1950); Donnellan, “Reference and Definite Descriptions” (1966)

Meaning as a rule of use

Strawson rejects representational theories of meaning, on which you understand an expression or sentence by looking at how it represents the world as being. No single theory explains all three touch-points plus motivating action: sense can’t do it all. He also thinks systematicity has been oversold.

Meaning as use

The meaning of an expression is a rule for its use. Not that meaning is determined by use: meaning and the rule of use are the same thing.

  • This is a social theory. All of Frege’s problems and points would make sense for a solitary individual; Strawson’s definition is essentially social.
  • Example: the meaning of “I” is the rule that it’s “used by someone to refer to themselves in speaking or writing.” It would make no sense to define it by pointing at someone.
  • HandoutSo rules of use are not Fregean senses. A sense determines a referent, but one rule for “I” yields a different referent for every speaker.

Expression, utterance, use

  • An expression is a type (the expression itself); an utterance is a token (a particular person saying it on a particular occasion).
  • A use is in between: two utterances make the same use of an expression iff they refer to the same object (or express the same truth conditions).

Against Russell: meaningful vs. true-or-false

Words don’t refer; people do. Take “The present King of France is bald.” Strawson says Russell conflates two questions:

  1. Is the sentence meaningful?
  2. Can it be used to express a true-or-false proposition when there’s no King of France?

“Meaningful” just tests whether there’s a coherent rule of use, i.e. whether the rules for the parts fit together, and this sentence has perfectly clear rules. Even so, a particular utterance of a meaningful sentence can fail to make a true-or-false assertion. (Strawson slides between “meaningful” and “significant.”)

Another problem: Russell’s theory applied to “the table is covered with stuff” says there’s exactly one table in the world, which is weird. HandoutThe speaker means the table that’s salient in context; rules for descriptions are partial and lean on context.

Presupposition vs. entailment

Two kinds of things are implied by (true if) a sentence S:

  1. entailments of S;
  2. presuppositions: background taken for granted.

Handout“It is Mary who won the race” asserts that Mary won and presupposes that someone won. Frege had seen this: “Whoever discovered the elliptic orbits died in misery” presupposes that someone discovered them.

Family-of-sentences test

p is a presupposition of S if p is implied by S and also by each of: It’s not the case that S; If S, then S′; It’s possible that S; Is it the case that S?

The idea is that p is implied every time you even put the sentence forward, without committing to its truth. Every member of the “present King of France” family commits you to there being a unique present King of France. That makes presupposition a good point against Russell, but not against Frege.

Donnellan: attributive vs. referential uses

  1. Attributive: if nothing is the φ, nothing has been said to be ψ. A detective standing over Smith’s mutilated body says “Smith’s murderer is insane,” meaning whoever did this.
  2. Referential: even if nothing is the φ, something has still been said to be ψ. At the trial, looking at Jones, someone says “Smith’s murderer is insane” and means Jones, guilty or not.

In the referential case, reference succeeds through the speaker’s intentions and the context, even if nothing is the φ. That something is the φ is a presumption, not an entailment. Handout“The man drinking a martini seems to be enjoying himself” can be true even if he’s drinking water from a martini glass and nobody at the party has a martini.

Lecture 9Kaplan: demonstratives

Kaplan, “Demonstratives” (1989)

Does natural language have a logic? The case of “any”

Russell and Frege thought sentences have truth values and so stand in logical relations. Strawson says natural language can’t be reduced to a formal language with well-defined truth conditions (“no exact logic”). Chomsky thinks you can have formal logic for syntax but not for semantics. E.g. a description doesn’t always have to be satisfied for you to use it successfully (the courtroom murderer from Lec 8).

Evidence that logic is built into language comes from the word “any”:

  • I don’t have any money. ✓
  • *I have any money. ✗

We’ve internalized some rule, and it’s tied to downward entailment. Positive contexts are upward-entailing; negative ones are downward-entailing (“I don’t have a pizza” ⇒ “I don’t have a pizza with anchovies”), and “any” is allowed in downward-entailing positions. With “every,” the restrictor is downward-entailing and the nuclear scope is upward-entailing: “Everyone who completes any assignment passes” is fine, but “Everyone who takes this class will get any grade” isn’t. So grammaticality is sensitive to logical properties, and those only make sense if sentences have truth conditions.

Compositionality as a problem for Strawson

Recall that a language is compositional iff the meaning of every complex expression is determined by its structure and the meanings of its constituents. Strawson struggles here, because two expressions can have the same rule of use but contribute differently to larger sentences. Handout“I” and “the speaker” are both used to refer to the speaker, yet “If I were silent and Snowball were speaking, I would be a grad student” and “…, the speaker would be a grad student” differ in truth conditions.

Character and content

Everyone agrees meaning splits in two. For Kaplan:

Character

A function from context to content. It’s set by linguistic convention, determines the content in each context, and is what a competent speaker knows. It’s close to the everyday notion of “meaning.”

Content

The proposition expressed by the sentence in a context. Its subject is the actual person, not a name or description.

  • Agreement and disagreement need the same content. A: “I’m a skeptic.” B: “No, I’m not a skeptic.” They failed to disagree: same character, different content.
  • Content is what gets composed, so it’s what makes compositionality work.

Context, circumstance, and possible worlds

Use possible-worlds semantics: a proposition is true in some worlds and false in others, and asserting a sentence narrows down the worlds.

  • The context fixes the interpretation of the sentence: who “I” is, what “here” is, and so on.
  • The circumstance is the situation (world) where we evaluate the resulting content as true or false.

The grad-student examples: “I am a graduate student” and “The speaker is a graduate student” agree at the actual world but come apart at other worlds. Donkeys: “If we called a tail a leg, how many legs would a donkey have?” Four, because we evaluate what we say at that circumstance, holding our meanings fixed. But “…what would the correct answer be, if someone asked there?” is five. HandoutLikewise, “If we all spoke French, how many legs would a donkey have?” Four, not “quatre.” So the proposition itself is purely objective: what we say is separate from the words we use to say it.

“I am here now”: logically true but contingent

Traditionally, a sentence is valid iff it can be derived from the empty set of premises, i.e. it’s true without investigation and necessarily true (“P or not P”). But “I am here now” is true whenever you say it, because the context of utterance is also the circumstance of evaluation. Yet it isn’t necessary: I could have stayed home today. So it’s logically true but not necessary. The proposition expressed could easily have been false.

What is a context? Semantics vs. metasemantics

Strawson says context is messy; Kaplan says it’s just “the input to character.”

Kaplan’s Principle 1: the referent of a pure indexical depends on the context, and the referent of a demonstrative depends on the associated demonstration. But this isn’t automatic. You can say “that” without pointing, and for “here” the size of the area (“I live here”) depends heavily on context. So Principle 1 is at best incomplete.

Handout

A Kaplanian context is a very partial description of a situation, a tagged list (speaker, audience, place, time, demonstrata). Kaplan doesn’t try to say how we get that neat list from a messy situation. He sets that aside as metasemantics (the study of the facts by which expressions have meaning), as opposed to semantics (the study of meaning).

Topic 1Topological spaces and bases

Munkres §12–13 · Lecture 1

Topology is the study of shape, and of the minimum structure needed to talk about shape.

Topologies and open sets

Topology

Let $X$ be a set. A topology on $X$ is a collection $\mathcal U$ of subsets of $X$ (its elements are called open sets) such that:

  1. $\varnothing, X \in \mathcal U$;
  2. any union of open sets is open;
  3. finite intersections of open sets are open.

A topological space is a set together with a topology on it.

Neighborhood

A neighborhood of $x \in X$ is an open set $U$ containing $x$.

Finer, coarser, comparable

Topologies $\mathcal U_1, \mathcal U_2$ on the same $X$ are comparable if $\mathcal U_1 \subset \mathcal U_2$ or $\mathcal U_2 \subset \mathcal U_1$. The larger one is finer, the smaller one coarser. They are equal if both containments hold.

Examples
  • Standard topology on $\mathbb R$: the open sets are unions of open intervals $(a,b) = \{x \in \mathbb R : a \lt x \lt b\}$.
  • Discrete topology on any set $X$: $\mathcal U = \mathcal P(X)$, so all subsets are open. Points are isolated: $\{x\}$ is a neighborhood of $x$ by itself.
  • Finite-complement topology $\mathbb R_{fc}$: the open sets are $\varnothing$ and complements of finite sets. Axiom 2 holds because a union just “fills in holes”; axiom 3 holds because a finite intersection just creates finitely many more holes.
  • Comparisons: discrete is finer than every other topology; finite-complement is coarser than standard.

Bases and subbases

Usually we define a topology by giving a basis.

Basis

A basis for a topology on $X$ is a collection $\mathcal B$ of subsets of $X$ such that:

  1. (B1) $\bigcup_{B \in \mathcal B} B = X$ (the basis elements cover $X$);
  2. (B2) if $B_1, B_2 \in \mathcal B$ and $x \in B_1 \cap B_2$, then there is $B_3 \in \mathcal B$ with $x \in B_3 \subset B_1 \cap B_2$.

The topology generated by $\mathcal B$, $\mathcal U_{\mathcal B}$, is the collection of all unions of basis elements.

Propositionproof ↓

A basis generates a topology. If $\mathcal B$ is a basis, then $\mathcal U_{\mathcal B}$ is a topology on $X$.

Subbasis

A subbasis for a topology on $X$ is any collection $\mathcal S$ of subsets of $X$ whose union is $X$. It generates a topology by taking all finite intersections of elements of $\mathcal S$, then all unions of those.

Examples
  • The singletons $\{x\}$ form a basis for the discrete topology.
  • Any topology is a basis for itself.
  • The open intervals $(a,b)$ form a basis for the standard topology on $\mathbb R$.

Metric spaces

Metric space

A set $X$ is a metric space if there is a distance function $d : X \times X \to \mathbb R_{\ge 0}$ such that for all $x,y,z \in X$:

  • $d(x,y) = d(y,x)$;
  • $d(x,y) \ge 0$, and $d(x,y) = 0 \iff x = y$;
  • $d(x,y) \le d(x,z) + d(z,y)$ (triangle inequality).
Metric topology

The metric topology on $(X,d)$ is the topology generated by the open balls $B(x,\varepsilon) = \{y \in X : d(x,y) \lt \varepsilon\}$.

Examples
  • Added$\mathbb R$ with $d(x,y) = |x-y|$: the balls are the open intervals, so the metric topology is the standard topology.
  • On $\mathbb R^2$ the Euclidean metric topology equals the product topology (see Topic 6).

Subspaces

Subspace topology

Let $X$ be a topological space with topology $\mathcal U_X$, and let $S \subset X$ be any subset. The subspace topology on $S$ is $$\mathcal U_{S} = \{\, U \cap S : U \in \mathcal U_X \,\}.$$

Examples
  • AddedIn $Y = [0,1] \subset \mathbb R$, the set $[0,\tfrac12) = (-1,\tfrac12) \cap Y$ is open in $Y$, though it isn’t open in $\mathbb R$.
  • $X = \mathbb R^2$ and $S$ = the Sierpiński triangle: the subspace topology gives something interesting.

Topic 3Closed sets, closure, and limit points

Munkres §17 · Lecture 3

Closed sets

Closed set

A subset $A$ of a topological space $X$ is closed if $X - A$ is open.

Theorem 17.1proof ↓

Let $X$ be a topological space. Then:

  1. $\varnothing$ and $X$ are closed;
  2. arbitrary intersections of closed sets are closed;
  3. finite unions of closed sets are closed.
Theorem 17.2proof ↓

Let $Y$ be a subspace of $X$. Then $A$ is closed in $Y$ iff $A = C \cap Y$ for some closed set $C$ of $X$.

Theorem 17.3proof ↓

Let $Y$ be a subspace of $X$. If $A$ is closed in $Y$ and $Y$ is closed in $X$, then $A$ is closed in $X$.

Interior and closure

Interior and closure

The interior $\operatorname{int} A$ is the union of all open sets contained in $A$: the largest open set inside $A$.

The closure $\bar A$ is the intersection of all closed sets containing $A$: the smallest closed set containing $A$.

So $\operatorname{int} A \subset A \subset \bar A$.

Theorem 17.4proof ↓

Let $Y$ be a subspace of $X$, let $A \subset Y$, and let $\bar A$ be the closure of $A$ in $X$. Then the closure of $A$ in $Y$ equals $\bar A \cap Y$.

Theorem 17.5proof ↓

Let $A$ be a subset of a topological space $X$.

  1. $x \in \bar A$ iff every open set $U$ containing $x$ intersects $A$.
  2. If the topology on $X$ is given by a basis, then $x \in \bar A$ iff every basis element $B$ containing $x$ intersects $A$.

Limit points

Limit point

If $A \subset X$ and $x \in X$, then $x$ is a limit point of $A$ if every neighborhood of $x$ intersects $A$ in some point other than $x$.

Theorem 17.6proof ↓

Let $A \subset X$ and let $A'$ be the set of all limit points of $A$. Then $\bar A = A \cup A'$.

Corollary 17.7Addedproof ↓

A subset of a topological space is closed iff it contains all its limit points.

ExamplesAdded
  • In $\mathbb R$: $\overline{\mathbb Q} = \mathbb R$ and $\operatorname{int}\mathbb Q = \varnothing$.
  • In $\mathbb R$, for $A = \{1/n : n \in \mathbb N\}$: $A' = \{0\}$, so $\bar A = A \cup \{0\}$ and $A$ is not closed.
  • In a discrete space every set is both open and closed, and no point is a limit point of anything.

Topic 4Hausdorff spaces and convergence

Munkres §17 · Lecture 3

Definitions

Hausdorff space

A topological space $X$ is Hausdorff if for each pair of distinct points $x_1 \ne x_2$ there are neighborhoods $U_1$ of $x_1$ and $U_2$ of $x_2$ that are disjoint.

Convergence

A sequence $x_1, x_2, \ldots \in X$ converges to $x \in X$ if for each neighborhood $U$ of $x$ there is $N$ such that $x_n \in U$ for all $n \ge N$.

$T_1$ axiom

$X$ satisfies the $T_1$ axiom if every finite point set is closed.

Results

Theorem 17.8proof ↓

Every finite point set in a Hausdorff space is closed. (So Hausdorff spaces are $T_1$.)

Theorem 17.9proof ↓

Let $X$ be $T_1$ and $A \subset X$. Then $x$ is a limit point of $A$ iff every neighborhood of $x$ contains infinitely many points of $A$.

Theorem 17.10proof ↓

If $X$ is Hausdorff, then a sequence of points of $X$ converges to at most one point of $X$.

Theorem 17.11Addedproof ↓

The product of two Hausdorff spaces is Hausdorff, and a subspace of a Hausdorff space is Hausdorff. (Munkres also includes: every simply ordered set is Hausdorff in the order topology.)

ExamplesAdded
  • Every metric space is Hausdorff: if $d(x,y) = r \gt 0$, the balls $B(x, r/2)$ and $B(y, r/2)$ are disjoint.
  • $\mathbb R_{fc}$ is $T_1$ but not Hausdorff: any two nonempty open sets intersect. In it, the sequence $1, 2, 3, \ldots$ converges to every point.

Topic 5Continuous functions and homeomorphisms

Munkres §18

Continuity

Continuous function

Let $X, Y$ be topological spaces. A function $f : X \to Y$ is continuous if for each open subset $V$ of $Y$, the set $f^{-1}(V)$ is an open subset of $X$. (Note that $f^{-1}$ means preimage here, not an inverse function.)

Factproof ↓

Checking on a basis or subbasis. If $Y$ has basis $\mathcal B$, it suffices to show that the preimage of every basis element is open. If $Y$ has subbasis $\mathcal S$, it suffices to show that the preimage of every subbasis element is open.

Theorem 18.1proof ↓

Let $X, Y$ be topological spaces and $f : X \to Y$. The following are equivalent:

  1. $f$ is continuous;
  2. for every $A \subset X$, $f(\bar A) \subset \overline{f(A)}$;
  3. for every closed set $B$ of $Y$, $f^{-1}(B)$ is closed in $X$;
  4. for each $x \in X$ and each neighborhood $V$ of $f(x)$, there is a neighborhood $U$ of $x$ with $f(U) \subset V$.

Homeomorphisms and embeddings

Homeomorphism

If $f : X \to Y$ is a bijection, $f$ is continuous, and $f^{-1}$ is also continuous, then $f$ is a homeomorphism. When a homeomorphism from $X$ to $Y$ exists, $X$ and $Y$ are homeomorphic.

This means $V$ is open iff $f^{-1}(V)$ is open, so $f$ gives a one-to-one correspondence between the open sets of $X$ and those of $Y$. It is the key notion of equivalence in topology.

Embedding

If $f : X \to Z$ is continuous and injective, and $f$ is a homeomorphism from $X$ onto $Y = f(X) \subset Z$ with the subspace topology, then $f$ is an embedding of $X$ into $Z$.

It’s much harder to show two spaces are not homeomorphic: you have to rule out every possible map.

Examples
  • $\mathbb R$ (standard) and $(0,1) \subset \mathbb R$ (subspace) are homeomorphic, via something like the tangent function: $x \mapsto \tan\big(\pi(x - \tfrac12)\big)$ from $(0,1)$ to $\mathbb R$.
  • Non-example. $f : \mathbb R \to \mathbb R_K$, $x \mapsto x$, is not a homeomorphism, because $K = \{1/n : n \in \mathbb N\}$ is closed in $\mathbb R_K$ but not in $\mathbb R$. This only rules out this one map, not every map. Added$\mathbb R_K$ has as basis all open intervals $(a,b)$ and all sets $(a,b) - K$.
  • Non-example. Let $f : \mathbb R \to \mathbb R^2$ be continuous and injective, with image a loop whose far end comes back and gets infinitely close to the crossing point $f(x_0) = (0,0)$ (it “slows down”). Give the image $Y$ the subspace topology. Then $f^{-1} : Y \to \mathbb R$ is not continuous. The preimage of a small neighborhood of $(0,0)$ in $Y$ is an interval around $x_0$ plus a tail $(t, \infty)$, so a small interval around $x_0$ alone is not the preimage of any open set of $Y$. So $f$ is not an embedding.
  • Let $X = \{a,b,c\}$ with the discrete topology. Sending $a, b, c$ to any three distinct points embeds $X$ in $\mathbb R^2$.
  • $\mathbb R_{fc}$ and $\mathbb R^2_{fc}$ are homeomorphic: any bijection $f : \mathbb R \to \mathbb R^2$ works, and one exists since the two sets have the same cardinality. If $U \ne \varnothing$ is open in $\mathbb R_{fc}$, then $U = \mathbb R - F$ with $F$ finite, so $f(U) = \mathbb R^2 - f(F)$ is open too. The same argument works the other way.
  • $\mathbb Z \subset \mathbb R$ and $\mathbb Q \subset \mathbb R$ (subspace topologies) are not homeomorphic. $\mathbb Z$ is discrete, so every point is isolated, but no point of $\mathbb Q$ is isolated: every open set in $\mathbb Q$ contains infinitely many points. If $g : \mathbb Z \to \mathbb Q$ were a homeomorphism, $\{0\}$ is open in $\mathbb Z$ but $g(\{0\})$ is not open in $\mathbb Q$.
  • $\mathbb Q$ vs. $\mathbb Q \cup \{\sqrt2\}$ (left without a conclusion in my notes). AddedThese are homeomorphic. The isolated-point argument can’t tell them apart, and by Sierpiński’s theorem every countable metric space with no isolated points is homeomorphic to $\mathbb Q$.

Finite products

Product topology on $X \times Y$

Let $X, Y$ be topological spaces with topologies $\mathcal U_X, \mathcal U_Y$. The product topology on $X \times Y$ is generated (as a basis) by the sets $U \times V$ with $U \in \mathcal U_X$, $V \in \mathcal U_Y$, which are called open rectangles.

Propositionproof ↓

Metric = product on $\mathbb R^2$. The metric topology and the product topology on $\mathbb R^2$ are the same. This is the standard topology on $\mathbb R^2$.

Examples
  • In $\mathbb R^2 = \mathbb R \times \mathbb R$, the open sets literally are unions of rectangles (without their edges).

Infinite products

Product topology on $\prod X_\alpha$

Let $X = \prod_{\alpha \in A} X_\alpha$, where $A$ is an index set. An element $x \in X$ is a function $x : A \to \bigcup_\alpha X_\alpha$ with $x(\alpha) \in X_\alpha$, written $x = (x_\alpha)_{\alpha \in A}$.

The product topology on $X$ is generated by the basis of sets $$\prod_{i \in I} U_i \times \prod_{\alpha \in A - I} X_\alpha, \qquad U_i \subset X_i \text{ open},\ I \text{ a finite set of indices}.$$ So all but finitely many factors must be the whole space.

Box topology

The box topology on $\prod_{\alpha \in A} X_\alpha$ has basis all products of open sets: $\big\{\prod_{\alpha \in A} U_\alpha : U_\alpha \subset X_\alpha \text{ open}\big\}$. For finite products it agrees with the product topology.

Factproof ↓

Projections are continuous. For each $\beta \in A$, the projection $\pi_\beta : \prod_\alpha X_\alpha \to X_\beta$, $(x_\alpha)_{\alpha \in A} \mapsto x_\beta$, is continuous.

Theorem 19.6proof ↓

Maps into products. Let $Z$ be a topological space and $f : Z \to \prod_{\alpha \in A} X_\alpha$ a function, with coordinates $f_\alpha : Z \to X_\alpha$. Then $f$ is continuous in the product topology iff every $f_\alpha$ is continuous. This is false for the box topology.

Examples
  • $\mathbb R^\omega = \mathbb R^{\mathbb N} = \prod_{n \in \mathbb N} X_n$ with each $X_n = \mathbb R$ (a countable product). The infinite open cube $(0,1)^{\mathbb N}$ is not open in the product topology, but $\prod_{n \le N} (0,1) \times \prod_{n \gt N} \mathbb R$ is. In the box topology, $(0,1)^{\mathbb N}$ is open.
  • AddedWhy 19.6 fails for the box topology: $f : \mathbb R \to \mathbb R^\omega$, $f(t) = (t, t, t, \ldots)$ has continuous coordinates, but $f^{-1}\big(\prod_n (-\tfrac1n, \tfrac1n)\big) = \{0\}$ is not open.

Quotient topology and quotient maps

Quotient topology

Let $X$ be a topological space, $S$ a set, and $q : X \to S$ surjective. The quotient topology on $S$ from $q$ is $$\{\, U \subset S : q^{-1}(U) \text{ is open in } X \,\},$$ and $q$ is called a quotient map. Think of this as pushing the topology forward from $X$ to $S$.

Quotient map

If $f : X \to Y$ is a map between topological spaces, $f$ is a quotient map if:

  1. it is surjective;
  2. it is continuous;
  3. the topology on $Y$ coincides with the quotient topology from $f$. That is, $V \subset Y$ is open iff $f^{-1}(V)$ is open.
Saturated set

$A \subset X$ is saturated (for $q$) if $A = q^{-1}(q(A))$, i.e. $A$ is itself the full preimage of a set, not just a piece of one.

Factproof ↓

Quotient maps via saturated sets. A continuous surjection $f : X \to Y$ is a quotient map iff $f(U)$ is open in $Y$ for every saturated open set $U \subset X$. This is the same as condition 3 above.

Examples
  • $f : \mathbb R \to [0,\infty)$, $f(x) = x^2$, with the subspace topology on $[0,\infty)$. Consider two choices of domain topology: (1) $\mathbb R$ standard, (2) $\mathbb R$ discrete. In both cases $f$ is continuous and surjective. For continuity in (1), the preimage of $(a,b)$ with $0 \le a \lt b$ is $(-\sqrt b, -\sqrt a) \cup (\sqrt a, \sqrt b)$. But (1) is a quotient map and (2) is not. In (2), $\{-1, 1\}$ is saturated and open, but $f(\{-1,1\}) = \{1\}$ is not open.

Quotients by equivalence relations

Quotient space $X/{\sim}$

Let $X$ be a topological space and $\sim$ an equivalence relation on $X$: $x \sim x$; $x \sim y \iff y \sim x$; and $x \sim y,\ y \sim z \Rightarrow x \sim z$. Let $[x] = \{y \in X : y \sim x\}$ be the equivalence class of $x$.

The quotient space $Y = X/{\sim}$ is the set of all equivalence classes with the quotient topology from $q : X \to X/{\sim}$, $q(x) = [x]$. Its open sets are $\{U \subset X/{\sim} : q^{-1}(U) \text{ open in } X\}$, i.e. sets of equivalence classes whose union is open in $X$.

Propositionproof ↓

$\mathbb R/\mathbb Z$ is the circle. On $X = \mathbb R$, define $x \sim y$ iff $x - y \in \mathbb Z$, so $[x] = \{x + n : n \in \mathbb Z\}$. Then $Y = X/{\sim}$ is homeomorphic to $S^1 = \{(x,y) \in \mathbb R^2 : x^2 + y^2 = 1\}$ (subspace topology), via the map induced by $f : \mathbb R \to \mathbb R^2$, $f(t) = (\cos 2\pi t, \sin 2\pi t)$.

Examples
  • In $\mathbb R/\mathbb Z$: $3.75 \sim 0.75$. Every equivalence class has a representative in $[0,1)$, and $0 \sim 1$, so it’s an interval with its ends glued: a circle.

Connected spaces

Separation

Let $X$ be a topological space. A separation of $X$ is a pair of nonempty open sets $U, V$ with $U \cup V = X$ and $U \cap V = \varnothing$.

Connected

$X$ is connected if it has no separation. A subset $S \subset X$ is connected if it is connected in the subspace topology.

Factproof ↓

Clopen criterion. $X$ is connected iff the only subsets of $X$ that are both open and closed (“clopen”) are $\varnothing$ and $X$.

Propositionproof ↓

$[0,1]$ is connected. $[0,1] \subset \mathbb R$ is connected.

FactAddedproof ↓

Closures of connected sets. If $A$ is connected and $A \subset B \subset \bar A$, then $B$ is connected. In particular, the closure of a connected set is connected.

Examples
  • $X = [0,1] \subset \mathbb R$ is connected.
  • $Y = [0,1] \cup [2,3]$ is not connected. Take $U = (-\tfrac12, \tfrac32) \cap Y$ and $V = (\tfrac32, \tfrac72) \cap Y$: they are nonempty, disjoint, open in $Y$, and span $Y$, so they form a separation.

Components and total disconnectedness

Connected components

The connected components of $X$ are its maximal nonempty connected subsets. The component of $x \in X$ is the union of all connected $C \subset X$ with $x \in C$.

Totally disconnected

$X$ is totally disconnected if its connected components are single points.

Caution: components are not necessarily both open and closed. AddedThey are always closed (by the closure fact above), but not always open. In $\mathbb Q$, for example, the components are single points, which are not open.

Examples
  • Any set with the discrete topology is totally disconnected.
  • The Cantor set $\mathcal C$ is totally disconnected. Intuitively, $\mathcal C = \bigcap_n C_n$, where each $C_n$ is a union of $2^n$ disjoint closed intervals, each of length $1/3^n$. To see that $\mathcal C$ is not connected, use $C_1 = [0,\tfrac13] \cup [\tfrac23, 1]$: $\mathcal C$ meets both pieces, so the two pieces cut down to $\mathcal C$ give a separation.

Path connectedness

Path connected

$X$ is path connected if any two points $x, y \in X$ can be joined by a path, i.e. there is a continuous $\gamma : [0,1] \to X$ with $\gamma(0) = x$ and $\gamma(1) = y$.

Propositionproof ↓

Path connected ⇒ connected. If a space is path connected, then it is connected. The converse is false, so path connectedness is the stronger condition.

Propositionproof ↓

$\mathbb R^n$ is path connected. $\mathbb R^n$ is path connected (hence connected).

Examples
  • Topologist’s sine curve (connected but not path connected). Let $\Gamma = \{(x, \sin(1/x)) : x \gt 0\}$ and $X = \bar\Gamma = \Gamma \cup (\{0\} \times [-1,1]) \subset \mathbb R^2$. $X$ is connected, since $\Gamma$ is connected and taking the closure keeps it connected. $X$ is not path connected: you can’t connect a point on the limit segment to anything else.

Local connectedness

Locally connected

$X$ is locally connected if it has a basis for its topology consisting of open connected sets. That is, for any neighborhood $V$ of $x$ there is an open connected neighborhood $U$ of $x$ with $U \subset V$.

Examples
  • $X = \{0, 1\} \subset \mathbb R$ is locally connected but not connected.
  • The topologist’s sine curve is not locally connected near the segment $\{0\} \times [-1,1]$: small neighborhoods there look like infinitely many separate strands.
  • Infinite comb (path connected and connected, but not locally connected): $$E = \big([0,1] \times \{0\}\big) \cup \big(\{0\} \times [0,1]\big) \cup \bigcup_{n \in \mathbb N} \{\tfrac1n\} \times [0,1].$$ It’s path connected because any two points can be joined by going down to the base, so it’s also connected. It’s not locally connected, because a small neighborhood of $p = (0,1)$ looks like infinitely many separate vertical pieces.

Open covers and compactness

Open cover, subcover

Let $X$ be a topological space. A collection $\mathcal V$ of open sets in $X$ is an open cover if $\bigcup_{V \in \mathcal V} V = X$. A subcover is a subcollection $\mathcal V' \subset \mathcal V$ that also covers $X$. You can remove sets, but you can’t change them.

Compact

$X$ is compact if every open cover of $X$ has a finite subcover, i.e. there are $V_1, \ldots, V_n \in \mathcal V$ with $V_1 \cup \cdots \cup V_n = X$.

A subset $Y \subset X$ is compact if it is compact as a subspace. Equivalently, if $\mathcal V$ is a collection of sets open in $X$ with $\bigcup_{V \in \mathcal V} V \supset Y$, then there is a finite subcollection $V_1 \cup \cdots \cup V_n \supset Y$.

Examples
  • $\mathbb R$ is not compact: one open cover without a finite subcover is enough. Take $\mathcal V = \{(a, a+1) : a \in \mathbb R\}$.
  • $\mathbb R_{fc}$ is compact.
  • AddedIn $\mathbb R_{fc}$ every subset is compact (same argument). So $\mathbb N \subset \mathbb R_{fc}$ is compact but not closed.

Compact sets, closed sets, and Hausdorff spaces

Factproof ↓

Closed in compact is compact. A closed subset $Y$ of a compact space $X$ is compact.

Propositionproof ↓

Compact in Hausdorff is closed. Suppose $X$ is Hausdorff. If $Y \subset X$ is compact, then $Y$ is closed. In a general topological space, compact does not imply closed (see the $\mathbb R_{fc}$ example).

Theorem 27.3proof ↓

Heine–Borel. A subset $Y \subset \mathbb R^n$ (standard topology) is compact iff $Y$ is closed and bounded, i.e. there is $R \gt 0$ with $Y \subset B(0, R)$. “Bounded” is only defined in metric spaces.

Compactness and continuous maps

Theorems 26.5, 26.6proof ↓

Suppose $f : X \to Y$ is continuous and $X$ is compact.

  1. The image $f(X)$ is compact.
  2. If also $f$ is injective and $Y$ is Hausdorff, then $f$ is an embedding.
Theorem

Brouwer, 1910. If $X$ is a nonempty, compact, totally disconnected metric space with no isolated points, then $X$ is homeomorphic to $\mathcal C$, the standard middle-thirds Cantor set in $[0,1] \subset \mathbb R$. Recall that $\mathcal C \cong \{0,1\}^{\mathbb N}$.

(⇒) $\mathbb R^n$ is Hausdorff, so compact $Y$ is closed. The balls $B(0,m)$, $m = 1, 2, \ldots$, cover $Y$. A finite subcover gives $Y \subset B(0, M)$, so $Y$ is bounded.

(⇐) If $Y \subset B(0,R)$, then $Y \subset [-R,R]^n$. Closed intervals $[a,b]$ are compact (Munkres 27.1, from the least upper bound property), and finite products of compact spaces are compact (Munkres 26.7), so $[-R,R]^n$ is compact. $Y$ is closed in it, so $Y$ is compact. ∎
